Making budgeting easier for college students

KLI asked our team to research opportunities for improvement in the financial sector. We focused on why college students struggle to budget. I managed the project and research, and we explored one design concept.

Role
Project manager & researcher
Team
9 UX designers
Platform

Timeline

Overview

Key Lime Interactive (KLI) is a UX research and customer experience consulting agency. This 16-week sponsored project explored how digital tools could improve financial literacy among college students.

Research focused on students' financial behaviors, challenges, and motivations to better understand how technology could support healthier financial habits. KLI asked us to research opportunities for improvement in the financial sector, so research was the core deliverable. We also explored one design concept.

The Problem

Students know budgeting matters. So why aren't they doing it?

College students face growing financial responsibilities, often without the knowledge or experience to manage them confidently. Budgeting can feel time-consuming, confusing, and difficult to maintain alongside school and everyday life.

Existing financial tools offer plenty of information, but don't always make it easy for students to understand their spending or take action.

How might digital tools make budgeting more approachable and motivating for college students?

Process

Milestone 1Understanding financial behaviors

What shapes students' relationships with money? The project began with secondary research, student interviews, and an exploration of online financial discussions. These methods helped uncover how college students learn about money, manage spending, and approach budgeting.

I led the literature review and developed the interview protocol, establishing a research foundation for the team's next activities.

Three patterns emerged:

Spending awareness

Students often underestimate everyday purchases and struggle to recognize how quickly expenses add up.

Financial confidence

Limited financial knowledge makes budgeting feel intimidating, especially for students managing money independently for the first time.

Motivation

Students understand the importance of budgeting but struggle to make it a consistent habit.

Key insight: Students recognize the value of budgeting, but knowing its importance doesn't make it easier to start.

Milestone 2Why aren't students budgeting?

A discovery workshop explored students' financial habits, spending behaviors, and motivations in greater depth.

I created and facilitated the workshop, using interactive activities to encourage students to reflect on their financial decisions and discuss what might help them budget more consistently.

Participants often spent more than they expected, especially on everyday purchases. Although they recognized the benefits of budgeting, tracking expenses felt like additional work.

Financial incentives generated interest, but participants were uncertain whether rewards alone would keep them engaged.

Key insight: Budgeting needs to feel manageable and worthwhile, not like another responsibility.

Milestone 3What would students actually use?

How can budgeting fit into students' existing habits? I designed and facilitated a co-design workshop where students explored potential solutions and identified the features they would find most useful.

Three priorities stood out:

Automation

Students wanted less manual tracking and fewer repetitive tasks.

Accessibility

Simple interactions, minimal setup, and understandable information were more appealing than complicated financial tools.

Familiarity

Students preferred budgeting within an app they already used, particularly their banking app.

Key insight: A familiar, automated experience could reduce the effort of budgeting without requiring students to change their routines.

Exploring two concepts

Research and ideation produced two potential solutions: a receipt scanner with financial rewards and an AI-assisted budgeting tool.

Concept testing with 12 college-aged participants explored the strengths and weaknesses of each.

Receipt scanner

Participants appreciated the simplicity and potential cashback rewards. However, many rarely kept receipts and worried they would forget to scan them consistently.

AI budgeting assistant

Participants liked the spending visualizations and the option to create automated or manual budgets. Concerns centered on trusting AI with financial information and understanding how the feature would work.

Preferences were evenly split between the two concepts.

Earlier research supported the potential of AI-assisted budgeting, particularly its ability to reduce manual effort and fit into students' existing banking habits.

Key insight: Automation addressed a major barrier to budgeting, but the experience needed to be simple, understandable, and trustworthy.

Milestone 4Exploratory concept & testing

To explore one opportunity, our team used the findings to create low-fidelity wireframes for an AI-assisted budgeting feature integrated into a mobile banking app.

Students could generate a spending plan using AI or build a budget manually, with visual breakdowns to help them understand their finances.

The wireframes were evaluated with 12 college-aged participants to understand usability, comprehension, and trust.

12 of 12participants trusted AI budgeting within a banking app
10 of 12appreciated the detailed budget breakdowns
9 of 12wanted more information about the features before using AI
6 of 12found aspects of the spending visualizations confusing

Testing showed that students were open to AI-assisted budgeting but wanted clearer explanations of how it worked, what their spending information meant, and how their money would be handled.

Key insight: Trust in AI wasn't enough. Students also needed transparency and control over their financial decisions.

Exploratory concept

Putting the research into practice

Beyond the research itself, our team set a goal to turn the findings into a design concept: an AI-assisted budgeting experience integrated into a familiar mobile banking app. Students can generate personalized spending plans from their financial history or create their own budgets manually.

The concept addresses four needs uncovered throughout the research.

1

Budget where you already bank

Budgeting lives inside the banking app, reducing the need to download, learn, and maintain another financial tool.

2

Choose AI or manual budgeting

AI-generated spending plans reduce manual setup, while a manual option gives students control over how they manage their money.

3

Understand your spending

Visual breakdowns organize expenses into categories, helping students recognize spending patterns and understand their budgets.

4

Know how the tool works

Feature explanations provide context around AI-assisted budgeting, helping students understand the experience before using it.

The final mid-fidelity designs bring budgeting into an existing routine, with an emphasis on simplicity, automation, and financial clarity.

The prototype flow

The interactive prototype walks students from the bank home screen through choosing a path, reviewing and editing an AI-generated breakdown, and confirming their plan.

Results

Research and testing narrowed a broad financial literacy challenge into a budgeting experience shaped by students' behaviors and preferences.

12participants evaluated the initial concepts, with an even preference split between receipt scanning and AI-assisted budgeting
12 of 12participants in subsequent testing expressed trust in AI budgeting when integrated into a banking app
10 of 12participants appreciated detailed budget breakdowns

Testing also revealed opportunities to improve feature explanations, spending visualizations, and transparency around AI.

The team delivered research findings, project documentation, and mid-fidelity designs to Key Lime Interactive.

What I learned

Research can challenge assumptions.

Students didn't just need more reasons to budget. They needed a process that required less effort.

Co-design brings new perspectives.

Giving students space to explore solutions revealed preferences for automation, familiar tools, and greater control.

One method doesn't tell the whole story.

Concept testing produced an even split, while earlier research helped clarify which features addressed students' underlying needs.

Leading a team requires clarity.

Clear responsibilities, consistent communication, and collaborative activities helped keep nine designers working toward the same goal.

Limitations & next steps

Limitations
Next steps

AI transparency needs refinement. Participants trusted the concept but wanted clearer explanations of how automated budgeting would work and how their money would be handled.

→

1Further refine AI transparency and test whether students understand how automated spending plans are generated.

Financial visualizations caused confusion. Some participants struggled to distinguish between current spending and future budget allocations.

→

2Refine the charts and validate comprehension through additional usability testing.

The concept wasn't tested over time. Our testing evaluated reactions to designs, not whether students would consistently use the tool or improve their financial habits.

→

3Test a functional prototype over a longer period to evaluate adoption, sustained engagement, and budgeting behavior.
